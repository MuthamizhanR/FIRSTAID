# [[Public Health Sciences|Public Health Sciences]] - Communication Skills


## Patient-centered interviewing techniques


|**[[Introduction|Introduction]]**|Introduce yourself and ask the patient their name and how they would like to be addressed. Address<br>the patient by the name and pronouns given. Avoid making gender assumptions. Sit at eye level,<br>near thepatient, while facingthem directly.|
|**Agenda setting**<br>**l**|Identifyconcerns and setgoals bydeveloping joint agenda between thephysician and thepatient.|
|**Reflection**|Actively listen and synthesize information offered by the patient, particularly with respect to<br>primaryconcern(s).|
|**Validation**|Legitimize or affirm thepatient’sperspectives.|
|**Recapitulation**|Summarize what thepatient has said so far to ensure correct interpretation.|
|**Facilitation**|Encourage the patient to speak freely without guiding responses or leading questions. Allow the<br>patient to ask questions throughout the encounter.|
|**Establishing rapport**|**PEARLS**|
|**Partnership**|Work together withpatient to identify primaryconcerns and develop preferred solutions.|
|**Empathy**|Acknowledge the emotions displayed and demonstrate understanding of why the patient is feeling<br>that way.|
|**Apology**|Takepersonal responsibilitywhen appropriate.|
|**Respect**|Commend the patient for coming in to discuss a problem, pushing through challenging<br>circumstances, keepingapositive attitude, or other constructive behaviors.|
|**Legitimization**|Assurepatient that emotional responses are understandable or common.|
|**Support**|Reassure patient that you will work together through difficult times and offer appropriate resources.|

|**Delivering bad news**|**SPIKES**|
|**Setting**|Offer in advance for the patient to bring support. Eliminate distractions, ensure privacy, and sit<br>down with thepatient to talk.|
|**Perception**|Determine thepatient’s understandingand expectations of the situation.|
|**Invitation**|Obtain thepatient’spermission to disclose the news and what level of detail is desired.|
|**Knowledge**|Share the information in small pieces without medical jargon, allowing time to process. Assess the<br>patient’s understanding.|
|**Emotions**|Acknowledge the patient’s emotions, and provide opportunity to express them. Listen and offer<br>empathetic responses.|
|**Strategy**|If the patient feels ready, discuss treatment options and goals of care. Offer an agenda for the next<br>appointment. Giving control to the patient may be empowering. Ask how they feel a problem<br>might be solved and what they would like to do about the plan of action.|

|**Gender- and sexuality-**<br>**inclusive history**<br>**taking**|Avoid making assumptions about sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, and<br>behavior (eg, a patient who identifies as heterosexual may engage in same-sex sexual activity).<br>Use gender-neutral terms when referring to the patient or the patient’s family (eg, “partner” rather<br>than “husband” or “wife”) upon first meeting the patient until the patient instructs otherwise or<br>uses specific pronouns. A patient’s assigned sex at birth and gender identity may differ. Do not<br>bring up gender or sexuality if it is not relevant to the visit (eg, a gender-nonconforming patient<br>seeking care for a hand laceration).<br>Consider stating what pronouns you use when you introduce yourself (eg, “I’m Dr. Smith, and I use she/<br>her pronouns”) and asking patients how they would like to be addressed. Also consider ways of being<br>inclusive (eg, ensuring correct name and pronouns are in the electronic medical record).<br>Reassure them about the confidentiality of their visits and be sensitive to the fact that patients may<br>not be open about their sexual orientation or gender identity to others in their life.<br>Remember: trust is built over time, and listening to and learning from patients about how they<br>would like to approach the topics discussed above is key.|
|**Cultural formulation**<br>**interview**|Identify the problem through the patient’s perspective. Ask the patient to describe the problem in<br>their own words, or how the patient would describe the problem to their family and friends.<br>Identify cultural perceptions of factors leading to a problem. Ask the patient to explain why they<br>think they are experiencing their problem.<br>Identify how the patient’s background influences their problem. Ask the patient about what makes<br>their problem better or worse. Investigate roles of family, community, and spirituality.<br>Identify how culture may impact current and future interventions. Ask the patient if they have any<br>concerns or suggestions about the current plan of treatment. If they do not want to follow medical<br>advice, investigate if there is a way to combine their plans with the standard medical regimen.<br>Identify possible barriers to care based on culture. Ask the patient if there is anything that would<br>prevent them from seeking care in a standard medical institution. Probe for explanations and<br>what may increase the chance of maintaining a good patient-physician relationship.|
|**Motivational**<br>**interviewing**|Counseling technique to facilitate behavior modification by helping patients resolve ambivalence<br>about change. Useful for many conditions (eg, nicotine dependence, obesity). Helpful when<br>patient has some desire to change, but it does not require that the patient be committed to making<br>the change. May involve asking patients to examine how their behavior interferes with their life<br>or why they might want to change it. Assess barriers (eg, food access, untreated trauma) that may<br>make behavior change difficult.<br>Assessing a patient’s readiness for change is also important for guiding physician-suggested goals.<br>These goals should be**S**pecific,**M**easurable,**A**chievable,**R**elevant, and**T**ime bound (**SMART**).|
|**Trauma-informed care**|Patients with history of psychological trauma should receive thorough behavioral health screenings.<br>Regularly assess mood, substance use, social supports, and suicide risk.<br>Focus assessments on trauma-related symptoms that interfere with social and occupational<br>function. Always be empathetic. Do not ask invasive questions requiring the patient to describe<br>trauma in detail. Ask permission prior to discussion.<br>Before the physical exam, reassure patients that they may signal to end it immediately if they<br>experience too much physical or emotional discomfort. Offer the presence of additional staff for<br>support. Psychological counseling may be indicated. Follow-up counseling is offered (or advised)<br>as appropriate. Remember**4 R**’s:**R**ealize,**R**ecognize,**R**espond,**R**esist retraumatization.|

|**Challenging patient and ethical**<br>**scenarios**|The most appropriate response is usually one that is**p**atient-centered, open-ended,<br>and empathetic; acknowledges the**o**bstacles in care; and**v**alidates emotions. It often<br>honors one or more of the principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and<br>justice. Appropriate responses are respectful of patients and other members of the<br>healthcare team. Consider thepatient’s**p**oint**o**f**v**iew.|

|Patient does not follow the medical<br>plan.|Determine whether there are financial, logistical, or other obstacles preventing the<br>patient’s adherence. Do not coerce the patient into adhering or refer the patient to<br>anotherphysician. Schedule regular follow-upvisits to trackpatientprogress.|
|Patient desires an unnecessary<br>procedure.|Attempt to understand why the patient wants the procedure and address underlying<br>concerns. Do not refuse to see the patient or refer to another physician. Avoid<br>performingunnecessary procedures.|
|Patient has difficulty taking<br>medications.|Determine what factors are involved in the patient’s difficulties. If comprehension or<br>memory are issues, use techniques such as providing written instructions, using the<br>teach-back method, or simplifyingtreatment regimens.|
|Family members ask for information<br>aboutpatient’sprognosis.|Avoid discussing issues with relatives without the patient’s permission.|
|A patient’s family member asks you<br>not to disclose the results of a test<br>if the prognosis is poor because<br>the patient will be “unable to<br>handle it.”|Explore why the family member believes this would be detrimental, including<br>possible cultural factors. Explain that if the patient would like to know information<br>concerning care, it will not be withheld. However, if you believe the patient might<br>seriously harm self or others if informed, you may invoke therapeutic privilege and<br>withhold the information.|
|A 17-year-old is pregnant and<br>requests an abortion.|Many states require parental notification or consent for minors for an abortion. Unless<br>there are specific medical risks associated with pregnancy, a physician should not<br>sway the patient’s decision for, or against, an elective abortion (regardless of patient’s<br>age or fetal condition). Discuss options for terminating the pregnancy and refer to<br>abortion care, if needed.|
|A 15-year-old is pregnant and wants<br>to raise the child. The patient’s<br>parents want you to tell the patient<br>togive the child upfor adoption.|The patient retains the right to make decisions regarding the child, even if the patient’s<br>parents disagree. Provide information to the teenager about the practical aspects<br>of caring for a baby. Discuss options for terminating the pregnancy, if requested.<br>Encourage discussion between thepatient andparents to reach the best decision.|
|A terminally ill patient requests<br>physician-assisted dying.|The overwhelming majority of states prohibit most forms of physician-assisted dying.<br>Physicians may, however, prescribe medically appropriate analgesics even if they<br>potentiallyshorten thepatient’s life.|
|Patient is suicidal.|Assess the seriousness of the threat. If patient is actively suicidal with a plan, suggest<br>remainingin the hospital voluntarily;patient maybe hospitalized involuntarilyif needed.|
|Patient states that you are attractive<br>and asks if you would go on a date.|Use a chaperone if necessary. Romantic relationships with patients are never<br>appropriate. Set firm professional boundaries with direct communication. Transition<br>care to anotherphysician if necessary.|
|A woman who had a mastectomy<br>says she now feels “ugly.”|Find out why the patient feels this way. Do not offer falsely reassuring statements (eg,<br>“You still lookgood”).|
|Patient is angry about the long time<br>spent in the waiting room.|Acknowledge the patient’s anger, but do not take a patient’s anger personally. Thank<br>the patient for being patient and apologize for any inconvenience. Stay away from<br>efforts to explain the delay.|
|Patient is upset with treatment<br>received from another physician.|Suggest that the patient speak directly to that physician regarding the concern. If the<br>problem is with a member of the office staff, reassure the patient you will speak to<br>thatperson.|

## Challenging patient and ethical scenarios** **_(continued)_


|SItuatIon|approprIate reSponSe|
|An invasive test is performed on the<br>wrong patient.|Regardless of the outcome, a physician is ethically obligated to inform a patient that a<br>mistake has been made.|
|A patient requires a treatment not<br>covered byinsurance.|Discuss all treatment options with patients, even if some are not covered by their<br>insurance companies. Informpatient of financial assistanceprograms.|
|A 7-year-old boy loses a sister to<br>cancer and now feels responsible.|At ages 5–7, children begin to understand that death is permanent, all life functions<br>end completely at death, and everything that is alive eventually dies. Provide a direct,<br>concrete description of his sister’s death. Avoid clichés and euphemisms. Reassure the<br>boy that he is not responsible. Identify and normalize fears and feelings. Encourage<br>playand healthycopingbehaviors(eg, rememberingher in his own way).|

Ask patient<br>direct, open-ended questions about exam findings and summarize patient’s answers<br>back to them. Ask if patient has any questions. Do not necessarily pressure patient to<br>leave apartner or disclose the incident to the authorities(unless required bystate law).|
|Patient wants to try alternative or<br>holistic medicine.|Explore any underlying reasons with the patient in a supportive, nonjudgmental manner.<br>Advise the patient of known benefits and risks of treatment, including adverse effects,<br>contraindications, and medication interactions. Seek guidance in reporting as procedures<br>and applicable law varybyinstitution and state.|
|Patient’s family insists on<br>maintaining life support after brain<br>death has occurred, citing patient’s<br>movements when touched.|Gently explain to family that there is no chance of recovery, and that brain death is<br>equivalent to death. Movement is due to spinal arc reflex and is not voluntary. Bring<br>case to appropriate [[Ethics|ethics]] board regarding futility of care and withdrawal of life<br>support.|
|A pharmaceutical company offers<br>you a sponsorship in exchange for<br>advertising its new drug.|Reject this offer. Generally, decline gifts and sponsorships to avoid any conflict of<br>interest. |Patient requests a nonemergent<br>procedure that is against your<br>personal or religious beliefs.|Provide accurate and unbiased information so patients can make an informed decision.<br>In a neutral, nonjudgmental manner, explain to the patient that you do not perform<br>theprocedure but offer to refer to anotherphysician.|
|Mother and 15-year-old daughter<br>are unresponsive and bleeding<br>heavily, but father refuses<br>transfusion because they are<br>Jehovah’s Witnesses.|Transfuse daughter, but do not transfuse mother. Emergent care can be refused by the<br>healthcare proxy for an adult, particularly when patient preferences are known or<br>reasonably inferred, but not for a minor based solely on faith.|
|A dependent patient presents<br>with injuries inconsistent with<br>caretaker’s story.|Document detailed history and physical. If possible and appropriate, interview the<br>patient alone. Provide any necessary medical care. If suspicion remains, contact<br>the appropriate agencies or authorities (eg, child or adult protective services) for an<br>evaluation. Inform the caretaker of your obligation to report. Physicians are required<br>bylaw to report anyreasonable suspicion of abuse, neglect, or endangerment.|
|A pediatrician recommends standard<br>vaccinations for a patient, but the<br>child’s parent refuses.|Address any concerns the parent has. Explain the risks and benefits of vaccinations<br>and why they are recommended. Do not administer routine vaccinations without the<br>parent’s consent.|

|**Communicating**<br>**with patients with**<br>**disabilities**|Patients may identify with person-first (ie, “a person with a disability”) or identity-first (ie, “a<br>disabled person”) language. Ask patients what terms they use.<br>Under most circumstances, talk directly to the patient. Do not assume that nonverbal patients do<br>not understand. Accompanying caregivers can add information to any discussion as needed.<br>Ask if assistance is desired rather than assuming the patient cannot do something alone. Most<br>people, including people with disabilities, value their independence.<br>For patients with speech difficulties, provide extra time for the interview. If their speech is difficult<br>to understand, consider asking them to write down a few words or ask them to rephrase their<br>sentence. Repeat what they said to ensure you understood it correctly.<br>For patients with a cognitive impairment, use concrete, specific language. Ask simple, direct<br>questions. Eliminate background noise and distractions. Do not assume the patient can read.<br>Adjust to how the patient understands best (eg, use hand gestures or ask them to demonstrate a task).<br>Ask patients who are deaf or hard of hearing their preferred mode of communication. Use light<br>touch or waving to get their attention. For patients who prefer to speak and lipread, eliminate<br>background noise, face the patient, and do not change your mode of speaking. Consider using an<br>interpreter when necessary.<br>As with other parts of a medical history, do not bring up a disability if it is not relevant to a visit (eg,<br>a patient in a wheelchair with an ear infection). Do not skip relevant parts of the physical exam<br>even if the disability makes the exam challenging.|
|**Use of interpreters**|Visits with a patient who speaks little English should utilize a professionally trained medical<br>interpreter unless the physician is conversationally fluent in the patient’s preferred language. If an<br>interpreter is unavailable in person, interpretation services may be provided by telephone or video<br>call. If the patient prefers to utilize a family member, this should be recorded in the chart.<br>Do not assume that a patient is a poor English speaker because of name, skin tone, or accent. Ask<br>the patient what language is preferred.<br>The physician should make eye contact with the patient and speak to them directly, without use of<br>third-person statements such as “tell him.”<br>Allow extra time for the interview, and ask one question at a time.<br>For in-person spoken language interpretation, the interpreter should ideally be next to or slightly<br>behind the patient. For sign language interpretation, the interpreter should be next to or slightly<br>behind the physician.<br>In cases of emergency, facilitate communication by any tools available (eg, friends, family, sketches,<br>interpreter apps) even though they do not comprise standard procedure otherwise.|
